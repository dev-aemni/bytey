## CELLULAR RESPIRATION AND ENERGY METABOLISM
Adenosine triphosphate (ATP) is the primary energy currency of the cell. Cellular respiration converts biochemical energy from nutrients into ATP through three main metabolic stages: glycolysis, the citric acid cycle (Krebs cycle), and the electron transport chain (ETC).
Glycolysis occurs in the cytosol of the cell and is an anaerobic process, meaning it does not require oxygen. It breaks down one molecule of glucose (a six-carbon sugar) into two molecules of pyruvate (a three-carbon compound). This process yields a net gain of two ATP molecules and two NADH molecules via substrate-level phosphorylation.
The citric acid cycle occurs inside the mitochondrial matrix. Before entering the cycle, pyruvate undergoes oxidative decarboxylation to form acetyl-CoA, producing one NADH and releasing one carbon dioxide molecule per pyruvate. Acetyl-CoA then combines with oxaloacetate to form citrate. The cycle processes two acetyl-CoA molecules per glucose molecule, yielding a total of two ATP, six NADH, and two FADH2 molecules, alongside four carbon dioxide molecules as waste products.
The electron transport chain is located on the inner mitochondrial membrane (cristae). NADH and FADH2 donate high-energy electrons to a series of transmembrane protein complexes (Complexes I-IV). As electrons move down the chain toward oxygen (the final electron acceptor), the released energy is used to pump protons (H+) from the matrix into the intermembrane space. This creates an electrochemical proton gradient. Protons flow back into the matrix down their concentration gradient through ATP synthase, a molecular motor that phosphorylates ADP into ATP. This process, called oxidative phosphorylation, generates approximately 26 to 28 ATP molecules per glucose molecule, resulting in a total cellular respiration yield of roughly 30 to 32 ATP.

## CARDIOVASCULAR REGULATION AND AUTONOMIC CONTROL
The human heart relies on an intrinsic conduction system to initiate and coordinate contractions, but its rate and output are regulated by the autonomic nervous system (ANS) and endocrine factors.
The sinoatrial (SA) node, located in the superior wall of the right atrium, functions as the primary pacemaker of the heart. It spontaneously depolarizes at an intrinsic rate of 60 to 100 times per minute. The action potential spreads across the atria, causing atrial contraction, and reaches the atrioventricular (AV) node. The AV node introduces a critical delay of approximately 0.1 seconds, allowing the ventricles to fill completely with blood before they contract. The impulse then travels rapidly down the Bundle of His, branches into the right and left bundle branches, and spreads through Purkinje fibers to trigger coordinated ventricular contraction from the apex upward.
Autonomic regulation is managed by the cardiovascular center in the medulla oblongata. Sympathetic stimulation via cardiac accelerator nerves releases norepinephrine, which binds to beta-1 adrenergic receptors on SA node cells. This increases sodium and calcium influx, accelerating depolarization and increasing both heart rate (positive chronotropic effect) and stroke volume/contractility (positive inotropic effect). Parasympathetic stimulation via the vagus nerve (Cranial Nerve X) releases acetylcholine, which binds to muscarinic receptors. This induces potassium efflux, hyperpolarizing the pacemaker cells, slowing depolarization, and decreasing the heart rate (negative chronotropic effect).

## ENDOCRINE REGULATION OF BLOOD GLUCOSE HOMEOSTASIS
Blood glucose concentrations are tightly regulated between 70 and 100 mg/dL through a negative feedback loop driven by endocrine cells within the islets of Langerhans in the pancreas.
When blood glucose levels rise, such as after a meal, pancreatic beta cells detect the increase and secrete insulin into the bloodstream. Insulin promotes glucose uptake into skeletal muscle cells and adipocytes by triggering the translocation of GLUT4 glucose transporter proteins to the cell membranes. Additionally, insulin accelerates glycogenesis (the synthesis of glycogen from glucose) in the liver and skeletal muscles, while inhibiting gluconeogenesis (the synthesis of glucose from non-carbohydrate sources) and glycogenolysis (the breakdown of glycogen).
When blood glucose levels drop, such as during fasting or intense exercise, pancreatic alpha cells secrete glucagon. Glucagon primarily targets the liver, stimulating glycogenolysis to rapidly release stored glucose into the blood. It also upregulates gluconeogenesis, converting amino acids and glycerol into glucose to maintain stable systemic levels and safeguard energy delivery to glucose-dependent organs like the brain.
